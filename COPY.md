## 2 · About

**Eyebrow:**
CHAPTER 02 — WHAT THE CLOTH SAYS

**Heading:**
Not a pattern. A language.

**Body:**

On the Nilgiri plateau, Toda people wear a draped shawl commonly transliterated
as *poothkuli*. Its coarse cotton ground carries bands of red and black
counted-thread embroidery, traditionally made by Toda women.

The embroidery is commonly transliterated as *pukhoor* or *pugur*. It is built
by counting the warp and weft of the cloth, without a traced design. At first
glance it can look woven rather than embroidered.

Its motifs draw on Toda life and the Nilgiri landscape. *Poothkuli* are worn at
important social and ceremonial occasions, including weddings and funerals.

**Plate captions:**
The bands run the length of the cloth.
Red and black thread on an off-white cotton ground.

## 3 · Construction

**Eyebrow:**
CHAPTER 03 — WHAT YOU CANNOT SEE

**Heading:**
There is no pattern to follow.

**Annotation labels (four, drawn to the margins):**

1. COUNTED ON WARP AND WEFT
2. NO TRACED GUIDE

**Pull line (progress 0.65):**
The back is also a face.

**Body (beside the macro plate):**

The coarse cotton ground lets the embroiderer count warp and weft directly.
There is no traced outline. The geometry is built with darning stitches, using
the weave itself as the guide.

Toda embroidery is reversible. Artisans take pride in the finish of both sides;
loops are part of the structure, not a hidden untidy back.

There is no single wrong side. The two faces do not look identical, but both
belong to the work.

## 4 · Motifs

**Plate captions:**

1. **Stacked lozenge**
   Colloquial name — Kaus Pohar

2. **Four-petal repeat**
   Colloquial name — Peersh Pohar
   Off-white petals open inside scarlet.

3. **Scalloped border**
   Colloquial name — Venki
   Curled and heart-like forms edge the band.

4. **Diagonal cross**
   Colloquial name — Flower
   Stepped arms turn through red and black.

5. **Diamond lattice**
   Colloquial name — Pohar
   Small cloth openings punctuate the crossings.

6. **Chevron pair**
   Colloquial name — Achak Pohoram
   Two narrow bands divide at a plain stripe.

## 8 · Closing

**Thesis (four lines, centred):**

Toda embroidery is not only the visible pattern.

It is the counting. The construction. The hours.

The cold place it comes from, and the hands that hold it.

We have been looking at the smallest part.

**Credits panel — column 1:**

MOTIF SOURCE
The six animated traces were derived from AI-generated motif plates made using
documentary photographs of real Toda embroidery as visual references. They are
exhibition reconstructions, not scholarly motif diagrams.

STITCH ARITHMETIC
334 traced units × 32 repeats × 6 bands = 64,128.

This is the exhibition’s counting model, not a measured or universal total for
a poothkuli.

METHOD
Built as a scroll-scrubbed exhibition. Every narrative movement is driven by
scroll and fully reversible. The only timed elements are the film-grain flicker
and the thread navigation's eight-second idle pulse.

PRIMARY DATA
This exhibition is informed by primary data and supporting research
material — including interviews, media documentation, and research findings
gathered or reviewed during the project. These sources shaped its narrative,
interpretation, and presentation of Toda embroidery.

**Credits panel — column 2:**

IMAGERY
The photographs of the real cloth used as reference for this project are
documentary. All imagery presented on this site is AI-generated, produced using
those photographs as visual reference. No image here is a photograph of a real
*putukuli*, a real artisan, or a real place.

This disclosure is part of the argument. A site about hidden authorship should
declare its own.
